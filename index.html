The book

Project Ariadne

Software Is a Human Problem Wearing a Technical Costume

One day, at the peak of the biggest project of my career, I did nothing at all. Everything I could touch was blocked on another team's API, an unmade decision, a database change only one person was allowed to perform. The project was later scrapped in its entirety. None of its wounds were technical.

Thirty-one chapters on the craft of code, the machinery of shipping, the people who do the work, the process theater that consumes them, and the decisions that actually determine outcomes. Every chapter opens with something that really happened, and argues against itself before it's done.

About

I'm a software engineer in Athens, and I've been writing software for a living since 2012. Most of that time has gone into public-sector platforms at GRNET, systems that ministries, universities and students depend on every day. Along the way I've built debt-collection systems, a school platform used across Greece, backend services for a sportsbook and payment integrations that moved real money. Mostly C# and .NET, with the queues, databases and dashboards that come with them.

The longer I do this, the less I believe the code is what goes wrong. The most expensive failure I ever worked inside had good engineers and sound technology, and it was scrapped anyway. What killed it lived between the teams: decisions nobody owned, handoffs nobody watched, ceremonies nobody believed in. Project Ariadne is my attempt to write down what that taught me, for engineers who are good at the technical part and have noticed that things still go wrong.

I'm a certified Professional Scrum Master, which the book has opinions about. Outside work I'm a retro-gaming enthusiast who still remembers the last corners of Need for Speed III.
